LE SALEVE

                  (near Geneva, Switzerland)

Not a trip report, but more of a "primer" for those unfamiliar
with the Saleve who might (or might not) want to make an
excursion.  The Saleve has a total of 450 routes, so here is
only a small portion, in general routes rated 6a to 6c that in
my opinion, are worth the effort.  Contents are as follows
(line numbers: WHERE=1, approximate):

This is a post-script added to the beginning.  In good weather,
a fun place to eat for not too much money is at the Bain Paquis
(the Paquis Beach).  It is near the Quai Wilson and is really a "scene"
on crowded days.  If you are a bit lazier, go there for breakfast -
very calm and 7 francs gets you a bowl of muesli with fresh fruit,
fresh-squeezed fruit juice, bread and jam, coffee.

WHERE: Near Geneva, but in France.  The departure point for
     most climbs is the very friendly bar/resto known as the
     "Refuge du Saleve".  Incidentally, patronize these guys -
     prices are reasonable, they are funny and helpful.  They
     have lent us water bottles (rather than making us buy a
     drink), even a motorcycle helmet when some asshole stole
     Sissi's.
          To get there, from Geneva or Annemasse, go to
     Collonges-sous-Saleve, stay left through town and keep
     going up the hill until you arrive at the tennis courts
     on the left.  Just after the courts is the parking lot
     for the majority of the climbs.
          By public transit take the H bus to the end and then
     walk (about 30-40 minutes).  Hitching is best done from
     the traffic circle in Carouge (rondeau de Carouge).  Sign
     essential.  Write: Saleve (Coin), so people will know you
     aren't going to the summit.  From Carouge, about 30
     minutes by bike.

GEAR AND INFO: The best place to find gear is Cactus Sports in
     Carouge (Geneva), right on the #12 tram line at the stop
     Place des Armes (where the street is closed to cars). 
     The address is:
          4, rue du Pont-Neuf
          CH-1227 CAROUGE
          (22) 300 30 01

     The official hours are:
          Monday, Tuesday, Thursday: 9:00 to 19:00, non-stop.
          Wednesday, Friday: 8:00 to 12:00

     Unless it is just my imagination, the store is often open
     wed. and fri. afternoons.  The owner, Bernard, is also
     author of the guidebook to the Saleve.  He has travelled
     a lot to the States and carries a lot of American gear. 
     If you need a replacement pick for your BD axe, this is
     the place.  I imagine he also speaks English pretty well.
          The guidebook is, of course, available here: LE
     GUIDE DU SALEVE, by Bernard WIETLISBACH, 1990 (about
     20.CHF)

CAMPING:  There is a campground open at the last hairpin
     before reaching the refuge du Saleve.  In addition, the
     Saleve abounds in good bivvy sites - lots of caves and so
     on.  Get water at the spigot near the refuge.

SEASON: Summer days can be hot and humid, in the fall it rains
     a lot, and in the winter some climbs occasionally have
     some ice on them.  In general the Saleve doesn't get much
     snow (altitude 650 meters at the foot of the climbs,
     1210m at the summit), but winter not the season to visit. 
     Summer afternoons and mornings are generally agreeable.

PARTNERS: Generally a hard place to pick up partners with the
     exception (perhaps) of Tuesday and Thursday evenings.

                      GENERAL DESCRIPTION

By and large, the Saleve is composed of excellent limestone,
very often extremely polished.  The Saleve is one of the
birthplaces of rock-climbing.  The French words "varapper,"
"varappeur" "varappe: ascension d'un couloir rocheux, d'une
paroi abrupte, en montagne" (Robert) (varappe: ascension of a
rocky couloir or abrupt wall in the mountains.  varappeur:
alpinist who does varappe") are named for a secteur of the
Saleve.  Tricouni nails were named for their inventor, F.
Genecand, nicknamed Tricouni by his climber friends at the
Saleve, author of such climbs as Tricouni Superieur, French
V+, around 1900!   Routes like the Nationale (III and IV) date
from the 1870s.
     All of this history adds to the ambiance, but also
detracts mightily from the coefficient of friction on the
classics.  I know lots of decent climbers fearful of routes
several grades below their level because of this.  If I were a
decent climber, I would be among them.
     Most routes are fully bolted.  That being said, "fully
bolted" at the Saleve has diverse meanings.  As the author of
the guide says: "The majority of the routes of a certain level
(TD and above) are very safe, even if one runs the risk of
taking some long falls.  If this perspective frightens you,
take along a few nuts to boost your morale."  Some climbs,
like Vieille France and Tablard, have reputations that exceed
their actual danger, while others, like Djinn le Malice,
probably deserve the reputation they have.  Sport climbing
that is sometimes quite sporting.  As a general rule, a dozen
quickdraws and perhaps a sling or two is sufficient to do vast
majority of the routes without getting overly scared.

                  THE CLIFFS AND THEIR CLIMBS

                           ********

                          FACE OUEST

     This is the impressive face that rises above the parking
lot.  It has a number of beautiful routes, only a handful of
which I have the guts, skill and strength to do, so I will
stick to those.


MORDILLO/GRAND DIEDRE (V-/V+).  Okay, I start by breaking my
     promise in describing a climb I haven't done.  I
     therefore mention in passing that this climb, not
     properly speaking on the FO, but in the dihedral that
     defines the limit between the FO and the Morgans Wall, is
     the easiest way up the FO (more or less).  I have seen
     parties in it and it seems to be fully bolted, with
     plenty of room for big gear if that is your pleasure. 
     Supposedly nice.  I can't verify any of that from
     personal experience.

PETER PAN (6b+, 6b+).  Pitch one is completely contrived (skip
     it).  Pitch two is way athletic, but impressive.  I had
     to rest on gear.  It wasn't pretty friends.  My rating:
     6b, 6b/A0.  On the other hand, this isn't where you are
     going to scare yourself to death - the bolts on second
     pitch are mercifully close together.  If you've got the
     juice, this is probably really nice, if you have wimpy
     little forearms like mine, PP is as humbling as it is
     fatiguing.

MONTEE AUX ALPAGES (6b, 6b+, 6a, finish up the FO Classique,
     5+, 4+).  A personal favorite at the Saleve.  The first
     pitch is a bit contrived, but nice and delicate slab
     climbing, occasionally a bit polished.  The second pitch
     is the best pitch in its grade (best pitch?) that I have
     done at the Saleve.  It is on average slightly
     overhanging, but less strenuous than it looks.  I don't
     think there are any no-hands rests on it until after the
     crux (where there is a nice one).  The moves are
     consistently interesting, with a take-a-deep-breath-and-
     don't-look-too-closely-at-the-bolt exit.  Beautiful.  I
     found the third pitch under-rated the first time I did
     the climb.  My brother (and partner for the day) told me
     I complain too much.  Having repeated the route, I guess
     he's right.  It's a nice pitch (but is it really 6a? 
     Enquiring minds...) that brings you into the main corner
     of the FO.  Don't belay too early - go to the top of the
     corner and out the giant bulge to an excellent belay. 
     Two moderate pitches lead to the top.

FO CLASSIQUE (5, 6b, 6a, 5+, 4+).  The easiest way to the top. 
     A classic (of course), though not especially sustained. 
     Pitch one is unremarkable, and perhaps a start via Chatte
     Penelop (5) gives a better access to the belay at the
     same grade.  The crux pitch is neither sustained nor
     strenuous, though once the grade eases off it has been
     known to draw complaints about the distance to the next
     bolt!  As the man said, if this frightens you... (there
     is indeed a good crack for gear, but if you pulled the
     crux, you've done the route).  From here this goes
     directly up from the belay, though a nice variant is to
     traverse a bit left into the short 6a overlap.  A word to
     the wise - put the position of the pin on top of the
     overlap well in your mind since it is hard to see from
     below but will greatly ease your mind.  Take the crack up
     to the same belay above the big bulge as for MONTEE. 
     This second part of the pitch is only 5, but really
     impressive.  Big air between your legs in the dihedral,
     big air onto the un-inviting slab below should
     concentration lapse (I've never heard of someone actually
     falling here, though, so no worries).  From there finish
     out the dihedral on a 5+ and finally a short exit pitch. 
     If you are prepared to run it out on easy ground and skip
     a couple of bolts you can combine the last two pitches
     without excessive rope drag.
          There are many variants for the exit (leaving mostly
     from the second to last belay) should you be tempted.


PETIT FUMEE, the lazy way (6b+, 6c, 6b+, finish out the FO
     Classique).  The lazy way?  Well, the route is actually a
     horridly steep 7b on pitch 4 which I was happy to skip
     since I would have been unhappy to have had to second it
     on jumars).  In any case, this is splendid climbing for
     the first two pitches.  The first pitch is a 6b+ that
     seems unrelenting.  Then, at the end, you finally get to
     the crux nice and tired.  There's a rest just before the
     crux and I was going up and down trying to find the easy
     way up.  Finally Steph yelled up: "If you can't figure
     out where a route goes, that means it goes dead on, it's
     just hard."  I found this to be correct on both counts. 
     The rock looks a bit friable, but everything holds well,
     and the bolts are always planted in good-looking rock. 
     Pitch two is nasty.  I didn't lead it.  The easy part at
     the bottom isn't (easy). It's followed by a low-angle
     slab that isn't (low-angle).  Steph ended up resting on
     gear.  I asked "Is it strenuous or technical?"  "Yeah." 
     Don't miss the clip on the traverse!  Pitch 3 is
     unremarkable.  I say this out of spite because I fell. 
     It looked easy, it felt easy, but the easiest of all
     seemed to be popping off.  The wimp finish is up the FO
     Classique.  That suited us perfectly.

Descent from the FO is normally by the Etiollets trail.  This
will take you by the climb POISSON D'AVRIL (6b) (just after a
cave/overhang with a chain goin out it).  If the body is still
willing, this is a fun climb with a strange exit to the first
belay ledge.

                       PAROI DES MORGANS

TABLARD (6b) has a reputation for being dangerous if you pump
     out on the crux.  This was my first climb at the Saleve
     and have only the vague memory of hanging out feeling my
     strength drain while trying to get up the courage to go
     for the crux.  Anyway, it is quality climbing and I
     didn't feel it was exceptionally run-out, but not for the
     faint of heart (everyone who doesn't fall on it says the
     run-out is reasonable, those who take the dive, however,
     have varied opinions).

MORGANS (6a).  This is one of the mega-classics of the Saleve. 
     This is probably due to the fact that the number of nice
     6a is rather limited.  This means that the exit moves on
     the Morgans are also polished to perfection.  Here the
     reputation is one of a casual outing, but for me the
     polish means that this is also not for the faint of heart
     (but then I am a granite fan, so I am pretty ill at ease
     with this polished limestone).  That being said, those
     who actually don't make the climb, generally get turned
     back lower down on less-polished more technical moves at
     the bottom, so don't be deterred by my cowardly comments!

VIEILLE FRANCE (6b).  When, after having done Tablard, I said
     I had done Vieille France, the response was "Tu aimes les
     effrayeurs" (you like the scary ones).  This apparently
     is because, before being retro-bolted, Vieille France had
     one of the nastiest falls at the Saleve.  The distance of
     the crux bolt above the slab/corner below was still
     enough for your intrepid reporter chicken out and grab
     the sling to make the clip (I may not be brave, but I've
     never been injured in 20 years of climbing either). 
     Well, what do I know about the climbing except that I was
     gripped and thought it was hard and strenuous, but I am
     undoubtedly exaggerating a bit.

Just down from here is the ROCHE FENDUE, the giant detached
block with numerous hard routes on it [La Saga de Chablotin
(??), Le Dandy (7c), Geant Jones (8a+), Un Bel Ete (8a)].  I
don't climb here.  Inside the large chimney of the Roche are
some moderate climbs of dubious quality.  The right-hand ridge
of the Roche Fendue is home to one of the easier classics of
the Saleve, the ARETE DES ETIOLLETS (5+, direct, otherwise
5-).  Here easy climbing leads to the summit of the Roche
Fendue, and then one fords the giant crack separating it from
the main wall.  As the guidebook says: Nice route, with a
picturesque section: the crack passage.  Three solutions exist
to cross it"
     - jumping: the rapid but impressive solution
     - climbing: much less rapid, but in the end just as
          impressive
     - tyrolien traverse: the slow solution, but to tell the
          truth, ever more impressive.

                       LES BUTTIKOFFERS


     Like the FO, the "Buttis" has a short approach (10
minutes?) and a certain amount of loose rock in the upper
reaches.  It also has a considerable number of more moderate
climbs than the FO.  This all makes for a very crowded wall on
a summer evening and a not negligible stonefall danger (not to
be exaggerated, but real).  As a general rule, most routes are
two pitches long and can be descended on rappel.  For those
climbs with descent via the Vires (ledges) Buttikoffers, watch
out.  This descent "path" is far more frightening than the
vast majority of the routes.  Follow the chains, railings, and
chopped holds, consider keeping your shoes on.


DIEDRE DES BUTTIKOFFERS (Butti Dihedral) (6c-, 6b, 6b).  This
     climb marks the left-hand edge of the wall and climbs the
     huge left-facing dihedral.  It doesn't see many ascents
     and you will have the impression of having the cliff to
     yourself even on the most crowded days.  Nevertheless the
     excursion is well worth it.  Be warned, though, it is
     steeper and above all more strenuous than it looks. 
     Descent by the Vires.

FAUX FIL (6c, 6b).  Starts in the Buttikoffers Dihedral and
     goes out the slightly overhanging wall to the slab. 
     Follow this up to under the overhang (bulge) visible from
     the bottom, pull it, and then climb the face above.  The
     guidebook suggests belaying above the second overhang,
     while we belayed below it.  The advantage is that the
     first pitch isn't so long and strenuous.  The
     disadvantage is that the second pitch is long and
     strenuous.  Anyway, the climbing is nice, the middle of
     the first pitch is a bit run out, and the overhangs are
     easier than they look (while the rest...).  A good
     excursion.

VOYAGE AU BOUT DE LA NUIT (6b+, 6b+, 6b+).  A personal
     favorite and not to be missed.  I prefer the direct start
     (INCESTE DE CITRON).  This is also 6b+ but a bit more
     difficult and more sustained, while the normal start has
     only one hard move on it (well out from the bolt I might
     add).  The second pitch is a long traverse, but the
     second is not going to fall off into space or 8a (which
     amounts to the same thing for most of us).  Watch out for
     rope drag on the second pitch: it's imperative to sling
     long in the grooves that you traverse.  This pitch
     traverses almost the entire cliff and never rises
     abruptly once on the traverse.  Above all don't take the
     line of beige bolts in the groove as some lost neophyte
     climbers have done, massive rope drag resulting.  Not
     that I speak from personal experience or anything :-). 
     The third pitch looks loose and not worth the voyage, but
     the climbing improves as you go up and, ultimately, is a
     nice pitch.  Despite the official rating, the pitches
     decrease in steepness and difficulty as one rises.  The
     climb continues up the upper wall at 6c with some way
     run-out hooks moves (I haven't done it).

Two last notes RE the Buttis.  A good 6a outing is to combine
MOORE with LA ROSE DES SABLES - two pitches of 6a that bring
you to the second belay of VOYAGE.  Also, from the common
belay of MOORE and the first pitch of LA ROSE, a route parts
slightly to the right up a groove traversed by the VOYAGE. 
This route is not in the guide, but is an enjoyable and well-
equipped pitch in the 6a+ to 6b range.

                    BONHOMME ET ETOURNELLES

For an afternoon (or day) away from the crowds, hike up to the
Bonhomme.  By far the best approach, and much faster than the
Etiollets trail, is to park up the hill from the refuge at the
parking du Coin (at a three way intersection where the road
goes up to La Croisette).  Take the excellent Orjobet trail
abut 15 min. to the Chafardon trail, and take this to the
cliff.  A total of about 30 minutes takes you to the
amphitheatre between the two cliffs.  Follow the trail up a
bit along the left side to see the not to be missed Trou de la
Mule.  This cave cuts through the wall and exits on the other
side via a hole just big enough to pass through.  This is the
main descent route from the NATIONALE (q.v.).  The cave can be
a bit dirty, but can't be beat as a bivvy spot (except for the
approach).

The climbs here are normally one or two pitches, and don't
need much commentary since the essential can be seen from the
ground.  The climbs on the right-hand wall of the Bonhomme are
all 6a to 6c and all worth the outing.  A good portion (far
right) can top-roped with a single 50m rope.  The climbs on
the Etournelles are of the same genre, but I feel compelled to
signal one, DALL BATT (6a+), up the obvious prow to the left
of the main crack.  It's rude as 6a goes.  I only say this, of
course, because I couldn't figure out the last move for the
life of me.  My partner (of the same, perhaps lesser ability)
cruised it in about 2 second and couldn't figure out what my
problem was, but I stick to my guns - it's hard.

                          THE CANAPE

Extremely short access (5-10 minutes), short routes (15-20
meters, 50 meter rope always sufficient for descent on
rappel).  This is a fun place with about 60 routes from 5 to
7a+ and some new ones that are perhaps harder.  If you can
climb at least 5+ and you aren't bored by things under 7a, you
should find plenty to do.  Since all routes can be seen from
the bottom in their entirety, I think it is superfluous to
signal particular ones.  For you Coloradans, this is somewhat
like Shelf Road, though a greyer, more weathered limestone.  I
will just point out that two climbs that are not rated in the
guidebook (Larmes a Sandrine, Martien Pecheur) are about 6a/6b
and no worse or better than others in their grade.

                         The Nationale

This is the classic of the classics.  It combines the
Schmolitz (2, 1878), Pas d'Aral (3-, 1875)), Guttinger (3,
1878), Cliche (3-, ????), and the Boite aux Lettres (3-,????). 
A million (ok, several) variants exist.  The most fun, without
significantly increasing the difficulty, but significantly
increasing the aesthetic value, is as follows.
     First, leave the refuge and head up into the gravel pit,
follow it along it's left side (as you look at it), until the
trail cuts across it at the top, following a cable across the
top of the gravel pit.  A sandy step up leads back into the
trees, and along a path.  Follow the blue arrows until you
arrive at an exposed slab that overlooks the parking lot. 
Traverse this going right, and then up a short groove to the
large ledge, and through the woods to the buttress of the
upper face.  Here the true Nationale stays right up the grassy
ledges (III) with plenty of bolts, but it's nicer to climb
straight up to the large leaning tree by any one of the cracks
directly below it (IV by any of them).  Continue up the large
chimney Guttinger (III) to the top.  Follow the ledge to the
obvious ramp going up and left (Cliche).  Instead of following
this, however, cut right onto the exposed face with immense
buckets (IV) and follow this to a large detached block with a
pin that says "sauvetage" on it.  Turn the block to the left
(III) and then angle right and up the final slabs (III)  with
excellent exposure to the exit and then the summit of the
Aiguille de la Mule.  From here you can jump across (I don't)
the huge crack, or descend down a path until a small saddle,
from which you can downclimb about 3 meters into the gorge and
from there climb the large buttress (III-) of the Boite aux
Lettres.  Follow the path through the woods to the wall above
(Paroi de la Lamin) and take this to the right until the end
where it meets the descending wall that borders the large
gully.  Here, stay rather close to the corner (high point) and
follow the holds polished by generations of Genevans through
the Trou de la Mule (II).  This is small (you won't pass with
a backpack on) and therefore hard to see - don't go too high
or too far right.  This can also be desperate after a
rainstorm.
     This is a great route.  I've done it with a heavy pack.
I've done it in a few hours climbing individual pitches.  I've
done it in running shoes on the spur of the moment after work
during a brief clearing in the weather in 1.5 hours round trip
from Carouge via bicycle, trying to beat the rain and night. 
It is also where Veronique fell to her death while crossing
the gravel pit.  I was talking to her when she slipped, I was
the first on the scene at the bottom - images that one doesn't
forget soon if ever, that come back strong when I climb the
Nationale, especially when I am there alone.
