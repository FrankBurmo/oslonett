Top Ten Activities in San Jose

 For decades, visitors have used San Jose as a gateway to San Francisco 80 kilometers to the north, and to the Monterey Peninsula 97 kilometers to the south.

Now, thanks to an eight-year, $1.5 billion investment to revitalize the city's downtown, fans of the World Cup USA '94 games at Stanford Stadium will find plenty to do just minutes away within the San Jose metropolitan area. With the advantage of a location on the southern, sunny tip of the San Francisco Bay, San Jose visitors enjoy nearly 300 days of sunshine each year.

Here are the San Jose Convention & Visitors Bureau's top ten suggestions of things to see and do (in alphabetical order):

  • 1. Egyptian Museum
  • 2. Flea Market
  • 3. Great America
  • 4. Nightlife/Performing Arts
  • 5. Park/Garden Visit
  • 6. San Jose Historical Museum
  • 7. San Jose Museum of Art
  • 8. Tech Museum of Innovation
  • 9. Winchester Mystery House
  • 10. Winery Tour

1. Egyptian Museum

 Featuring the largest exhibit of ancient Egyptian antiquities on the West coast, the Rosicrucian is an archaeologist's delight with its mummies, ancient artifacts and an underground tomb.

2. Flea Market

The ultimate stop for bargain hunters, San Jose's flea market is the largest open-air market in the U.S., featuring 2,700 booths and 35 restaurants on 120 acres. Equal parts rummage sale and portable shopping mall, the flea market offers merchandise ranging from fresh produce and live plants to furniture, socks and rugs.

3. Great America

This 100-acre family theme park offers 33 rides and spectacular Broadway-style shows. In addition, Great America's Pictorium Theater ranks as one of five theaters in the world equipped to exhibit films in three-dimensional, IMAX (super-large) format.

4. Nightlife/Performing Arts

This family-oriented city is not normally thought of as a night owl's town, yet San Jose's concentrated five-block area of mega-nightclubs practically crackles with dancing energy on weekend nights. On the other hand, those whose energy levels dictate less strenuous activity can keep nocturnal company with the city's world-class performing arts organizations: San Jose Symphony Orchestra, Civic Light Opera, Opera San Jose, San Jose Repertory Theatre, and San Jose Cleveland Ballet.

5. Park/Garden Visit

Reminders of a time 50 years ago when the city was known as "The Garden City in the Valley of Heart's Delight," today's 125 parks and public gardens provide an oasis of green for outdoor enthusiasts to horseback ride, jog, hike, bike, or take a leisurely stroll.

6. San Jose Historical Museum

A 16-acre outdoor museum in Kelley Park, this collection of faithfully reproduced homes, businesses, and other historical landmarks presents the city's rich past as it existed near the close of the 19th century.

7. San Jose Museum of Art

In 1991, the museum celebrated the opening of a new 45,000-foot addition to its original facility, a 1892 Richardsonian Romaneque-style building. Just in time for World Cup USA '94 , this contemporary art museum will mount the first of four successive multi-million-dollar exhibitions drawn from the permanent collection of the Whitney Museum of Art in New York, the result of an unprecedented collaboration. To be installed in May, 1994 and continue for eight years, the long-term loan features 20th century American art.

8. Tech Museum of Innovation

Named one of the top 10 attractions to open in the United States in 1990, this museum provides insight into San Jose's most famous industry, offering visitors a fun, interactive look into the world of high technology.

9. Winchester Mystery House

This beautiful, yet bizarre mansion is a 160-room Victorian filled with such oddities as stairs that lead to the ceiling, doors that open into walls, and legends of ghosts and spirits.

10. Winery Tour

While nearly 50 award-winning wineries can be found in the surrounding area, two are within San Jose city limits--J. Lohr Winery and Mirassou Vineyards. Free tastings, outdoor concerts, harvest festivals and special dinners are just a sample of their offerings.

What's New in San Jose

 A menu of new developments in San Jose will ensure that the Capital of Silicon Valley continues to build upon a renaissance that began in 1988. Here are some exciting attractions that are sure to draw area residents and visitors alike to the largest city in Northern California and its environs:

Largest Outlet Mall in the West Set to Open in Late September

The Great Mall of the Bay Area, California's newest outlet mall, will be the largest outlet mall west of the Mississippi, and the largest shopping center to open in the United States this year. The 1.5 million-square-foot project, located in Milpitas ten minutes away from San Jose International Airport, is a $130 million conversion of a former Ford automobile assembly plant.

Anchored by eleven major stores, the mall will feature more than 210 manufacturer and off-price specialty tenants, including Old Navy (The Gap outlet), Media Play, Supersports USA, Coliseum (the B.U.M. Equipment outlet), Marshalls Superstore, Burlington Coat Factory, Linens N Things, and many more.

Rounding out the mix will be a 750-seat food court, eight full-service restaurants and Wonder Park, a 30,000-square-foot family entertainment center modeled after its popular namesake in Tokyo, Japan.

The mall will have uniformed greeters, a bus drivers' lounge and plenty of parking space for motorcoaches. Group tour incentives will include special coupon books and a pre-sell meal program. A Tour & Travel Marketing Manager will be hired to assist tour and travel industry professionals.

Whitney Museum of Art Comes to San Jose

Earlier this month, the San Jose Museum of Art celebrated the long-awaited first installation of masterworks selected from New York's Whitney Museum of American Art.

Over a period of eight years, the Whitney Museum of American Art will provide the San Jose Museum of Art with four successive large-scale exhibitions drawn from the Whitney's renowned permanent collection. Each exhibition will be displayed for 18 months.

This unprecedented collaborative relationship with the San Jose museum will enable the Whitney to make its collection accessible to audiences, students and scholars around the world, and to promote appreciation of the richness and diversity of American art. Currently, the Whitney Museum is only able to display a fraction of the 10,000 works in its permanent collection. The Whitney Museum, celebrating its 61st anniversary this year, is the leading institution devoted to 20th century American art.

Featured in the exhibition are works by such renowned artists as Georgia O'Keeffe, Edward Hopper, Alexander Calder and Jacob Lawrence. The exhibition is among the most complete and comprehensive showings of early 20th century American art on view in the world.

Established in 1969, the San Jose Museum of Art added a 45,000-square-foot wing to the original 33,000-square-foot facility in 1991. The long-term installations from the Whitney Museum occupy on-third of the available gallery space. The San Jose museum will continue to present exhibitions it organizes itself as well as those secured from other institutions.

Peralta Adobe and Fallon House Restored

Two of the oldest structures in San Jose got a new lease on life this winter when the Peralta Adobe and the nearby Fallon House were restored. An adjoining gift shop was added and opened in April.

Constructed during the 1790's by a member of the 1775 De Anza expedition, the Peralta Adobe eventually became home to the family of Don Luis Maria Peralta, one of San Jose's most successful and responsible leaders. Measuring only 21 by 41 feet, the humble brick structure is San Jose's oldest historic landmark and contains rustic furnishings and humble decorations appropriate to the period.

A few feet away stands the Fallon House, built around 1858 by Thomas Fallon, an officer in the U.S. Army during the War with Mexico and later mayor of the City of San Jose. The structure stood empty for a number of years after the Fallon family vacated it in 1878. Reopening as a hotel in 1900, the structure housed one of the most popular restaurants in the Valley frequented by celebrities such as John Steinbeck, Fatty Arbuckle and Charlie Chaplin.

San Jose Arena

The September 7 completion of the San Jose Arena marked a new milestone in the city's emergence as a major destination.

The new 450,000-square-foot, 20,000-seat arena is a welcome addition to the city's downtown skyline, especially for sports fans and concert-goers. The new arena not only serves as home to the San Jose Sharks national hockey team, but also hosts a number of headline entertainment events throughout the year.

Located in the heart of downtown, the arena is within walking distance of some of the city's finest restaurants, liveliest nightclubs, and family-friendly attractions. Also within walking distance of the 20-mile light rail system, the arena is accessible to all of the city's major hotels. Call 408/287-9200 for general information.

California History Abounds in San Jose

 San Jose's early beginnings reflect a cross section of California's colorful past. As the state's first civil settlement under Spanish rule, El Pueblo de San Jose de Guadalupe was founded on November 29, 1777 -- a time when New England colonists were just beginning their war for independence.

Soldiers under command of Spanish conquistador and explorer Gaspar de Partola, on a northward trek from Mexico, discovered a verdant California valley in 1769. Ensuing expeditions into what is now called Santa Clara Valley led to the day when 66 men, women, and children, headed by Lieutenant Jose Moraga, were ordered to establish the San Jose pueblo. Today, only a short distance from the Guadalupe riverbank site where that small band of colonists first gathered, City of San Jose officials serve a population of more than 803,000.

The purpose of the first settlement, as decreed by California's Spanish governor, was to raise crops and cattle to feed soldiers encamped at the Presidios of Monterey and San Francisco. San Jose became the first official Spanish colony with families since only the Franciscans, who founded the 21 California Missions under direction of Father Junipero Serra, had previously accompanied the early explorers.

Because of periodic flooding by the Guadalupe River, the colony was relocated to higher ground in 1797. At the northern boundary of this site, is the oldest structure in San Jose and the only one remaining from the Spanish colonial period. It is the Peralta Adobe at 184 West St. John Street, named after pueblo official Comisionado Luis Peralta, who occupied the adobe from 1807 until his death in 1851. Surrounded by modern high rises, the humble adobe is in a small park which was dedicated during the San Jose's bicentennial observance in 1977. The story of California's first settlement may be seen and heard via display cases featuring models of the pueblo, and push buttons activating recorded narratives.

Traces of the 1797 plaza are also found in what is called Plaza de Cesar Chavez on South Market Street, between San Fernando and San Carlos Streets. A historical marker in this park refers to a later period, in 1849, when San Jose was named the first state capitol.

California's first legislature met in a two-story adobe, on a site now occupied by the luxurious, 19-story Fairmont Hotel, which faces Plaza de Cesar Chavez. The conclave was to prepare for California's admission to the union in 1850. However, prior to actual admission, the capitol was moved several times before Sacramento was finally chosen as the permanent location.

Historic structures just north of City Plaza Park include San Jose's first federal building (1892), which now houses the San Jose Museum of Art, and St. Joseph Cathedral (l877). In June 1991, the San Jose Museum of Art completed a 45,000 square-foot addition to the existing Richardsonian Romanesque-style building. St. Joseph Cathedral completed a $17 million renovation in October 1990. Four blocks east is San Jose State University (l857), California's first institution of higher learning, with some 26,000 students today. Most prominent among campus landmarks is an ivy-covered tower, built in 1910.

Also located downtown is St. James Square and the handsome Santa Clara County Courthouse (1868), originally built in the vain hope that the state capitol would be returned to San Jose. The structure has been in continuous use since its dedication, except for part of 1931, when fire daused collapse of the original third floor

Across the square is the oldest church still in use in San Jose. It is the wooden, Carpenter Gothic-style Trinity Episcopal Church, built in 1863, with the later addition (1879) of the oldest bell chimes in northern California.

Just to the north of St. James Square is the Hensley Historic District, an area of roughly one square mile which includes the largest collection of original Victorian residences in the South San Francisco Bay Area. Designated a Historic District by the state and federal governments and the City of San Jose, it features many residences which, having suffered the ravages of time since their proud beginnings in the 19th century, have been painstakingly restored to their earlier splendor.

A concentrated and lively overview of San Jose's past is offered to visitors at the 25-acre San Jose Historical Museum. The museum boasts a growing collection of both original and faithfully reconstructed landmarks, which recreate turn-of-the-century San Jose. In addition to private homes with 1890's furnishings, there are the Dashaway Stables, Empire Firehouse, a 1927 gas station, and an old water tank tower. A replica of the Pacific Hotel lobby houses exhibits and a gift shop. The adjacent O'Brien's Soda Fountain, which echoes its predecessors at three different downtown locations between 1878 and 1956, is open to the public.

There's also a unique 117-foot electric light tower, a scale model of the 237-foot original that straddled a downtown San Jose intersection from 1881 to 1915. The tower, designed as one of four intended to "light the whole town," was a project of a visionary newspaper publisher of the time, who sought public funding for his novel idea. The structure was, reportedly, a source of controversy because wild fowl flew into the tower's lights, and farmers claimed their chickens were kept from roosting at night. The dilemma was solved when the publisher's dream was literally dashed to the ground by a windstorm in 1915.

Other elements reflecting late 19th century life include: a print shop, a doctor's office complete with a dentist chair fitted with manacles to immobilize reluctant patients, and a replica of the Bank of Italy's San Jose branch.

A.P. Giannini, born in San Jose, founded the Bank of Italy in San Francisco in 1904, then opened the first branch in his hometown in 1910. After renaming his firm the Bank of America, Giannini moved corporate headquarters from San Francisco to San Jose's then tallest building, in 1929. The distinctive structure, still standing at the corner of First and Santa Clara Streets, continued to house the bank's main offices until 1969, when the world-renowned institution shifted its headquarters back to San Francisco.

While San Jose State University is California's oldest public institution of higher learning, the University of Santa Clara (1851), in the city of the same name, is the state's oldest private institution of secondary education. Of particular interest on this campus is the Mission Santa Clara de Asis (1777), eighth in the chain of those founded by the Franciscans.

A most unusual historical attraction in San Jose, and one that is on the National Register of Historic Places, is the Winchester Mystery House. This bizarre 160-room Victorian mansion was under continuous day and night construction for 38 years (1884-1922). The builder, who personally designed stairways ending at the ceiling, among many other architectural oddities, was the reclusive Sarah Winchester. Legends say she consulted with the spirit world for architectural direction after the deaths of both her infant daughter and her husband, the heir to the Winchester rifle fortune.

San Jose: ``The Garden City''

 Some 50 years ago when blossoming fruit orchards surrounded San Jose the city was known as ``The Garden City in the Valley of Heart's Delight.'' Today, San Jose's metropolitan area in the Santa Clara Valley is known as ``Silicon Valley,'' because of the great concentration of electronic and high technology industries, but beautiful and historic gardens continue to thrive.

Although the urbanization of San Jose has made it California's third and the nation's 11th largest city, 125 parks and long-established public gardens remain to provide greenbelts in both business and residential sectors. The greening of San Jose has always existed because of its mild year-round climate in a sunbelt location, just 50 miles south of San Francisco. Before noting existing garden sites for visitors to tour, attention is focused on the city's commitment to a master plan that ensures attractive settings for construction projects that will carry into the next century.

For example, major elements of a long-planned downtown renaissance were completed in 1988 with extensive landscaping an integral part of each project. Sycamore trees shading the new downtown transit mall, antique street lights, granite-paved sidewalks, and decorative The Pavilion, a distinctive, European-style retail plaza, has a garden ambiance with a trellised, two-level colonnade of greenery, fountains and palm trees. These features are intended to create a relaxed atmosphere for browsing among specialty shops or dining at cafes and restaurants, day or night.

In the final planning stages is the Guadalupe River Park, a ``ribbon of green'' to be developed along a stretch of the Guadalupe River, between San Jose International Airport to the north and the San Jose McEnery Convention Center three miles to the south. Trees and other plantings will landscape walkways, bridges, recreational areas, theaters, restaurants and museums, scheduled to open along the riverbank in the late 1990s.

As for existing green belts, none is older than City Plaza Park, which dates back to the city's origins in the late 1700s. A few blocks away is the commemorative ``City Garden Nursery'' in Pellier Park. Here, in 1856, French emigre Louis Pellier planted the valley's first prune tree cuttings, and unknowingly launched a multi-million dollar agricultural boom that reached its peak in the late 1940s.

Three miles south, in 150-acre Kelley Park, is the tranquil Japanese Friendship Garden. It evolved in the 1950s in response to President Dwight D. Eisenhower's call for ``people to people'' relations between cities of the United States and other countries. San Jose set aside acreage, and citizens of Okayama, Japan, one of San Jose's sister cities, made generous donations for the garden's cultivation and landscaping. Winding paths around ponds and over knolls, as well as a series of foot bridges, lead to a tea house where visitors may relax and enjoy refreshments. Large, brightly colored Koi (carp) swimming in the ponds add another note of oriental authenticity.

A short drive from Kelley Park is the 37-acre Overfelt Botanical Garden. Much of the park has been left in a natural state to serve as a wildlife sanctuary. Small lakes and marshy areas provide food and shelter for both resident and migratory waterfowl. One section is devoted to a Chinese cultural garden, made possible by donations from another San Jose sister city, Tainan, Taiwan. A massive, ornate gate stands at the entrance to paths that lead to a statue of Confucius by a reflection pond and to two pavilions placed on knolls that provide views of the serene setting.

A mile and a half west of downtown is San Jose's Municipal Rose Garden, where more than 5,000 plantings of some 150 varieties of roses have added to the city's garden image since 1937. Centered by a splashing fountain, the showy blooms are surrounded by green lawns and trees shading tables for quiet picnics.

Two blocks away, interesting gardens surround the Egyptian Museum & Planetarium, formerly the Rosicrucian Egyptian Musem. Palm trees, papyrus, an obelisk, sphinx and other statuary, plus a reflecting pool and murals serve to accent the stylized structures of Rosicrucian Park.

Another San Jose area garden of special interest is that of the nearby Mission Santa Clara de Asis, on the University of Santa Clara campus. The mission was founded in 1777, and many of the garden's 4,500 roses are classified as antiques by experts. Ninety-six varieties of trees and plants on the grounds include fig and olive trees planted by Franciscan friars in 1822. Other specimens of note are 100-year-old wisteria vines, a giant 123-year-old sumac tree, and more recent plantings of gifts from around the world including an Ethiopian rose and purple jacaranda from Brazil.

Some small orchards remain in the valley, and in summer, a colorful patchwork of apricots and other fruits are laid out on drying trays in the California sun. Additional evidence that some elements of San Jose's agricultural past still exist: both local residents and visitors drive to area farms to purchase seasonal fruits, wines, flowers and vegetables. At Christmas, residents flock to hillside tree farms to cut their own yule trees.

Wineries

 Despite San Jose's ranking as the nation's 11th largest city and its highly urbanized profile, the area features nearly 50 wineries producing fine quality varieties, many with worldwide reputations.

The wineries, one operated by fifth generation members of its founding family, attest both to San Jose's early history and the hospitable climate of the fertile Santa Clara Valley. Two wineries operating within San Jose city limits, Mirassou Vineyards and J. Lohr Winery, typify others in the surrounding countryside. The range is from small, family-operated enterprises to those with large, modern facilities. Each has a fascinating history, and vignettes about the respective owners, winemasters and production of special vintages.

Franciscan friars settling in the Santa Clara Valley in the mid-1770s raised grapes at Mission Santa Clara de Asis for sacramental wines. However, it was families with wine-making histories in Europe who established the vineyards that served as the basis for today's commercial production. One of the areas earliest vintners, who realized the potential of the valley's favorable climate of sunny days and cool nights, was Pierre Pellier. His family emigrated to California from the La Rochelle region of France in 1848.

Pierre joined his family in 1850, bringing carefully tended cuttings of several types of varietal stock. He is credited with introducing to the area such grapes as Folle Blanche, Grey Riesling and French Colombard from the Charente district of France. When the eldest of

Pierre's daughters married another French emigre and neighboring vintner, Pierre Mirassou, the Pellier-Mirassou vintner alliance was formed. This connection established the roots of one of America's oldest wine-making families, whose traditions are continued in San Jose today by members of its fifth generation. In 1990, Mirassou Vineyards celebrated over 135 years of operation, offering its premium varietal table wines and champagne to wine-lovers in both this country and France. Located in southeast San Jose, Mirassou Vineyards is open year-round for tours and tasting of its fine vintages.

In November 1989, Mirassou relocated its champagne operation from San Jose to the historic Novitiate Winery in nearby Los Gatos. Built by the Jesuits in 1893, the original winery building still stands as a fine example of a 19th century gravity-flow winery, now the home of the Mirassou Champagne Cellars. The Cellars are also open year-round for tours and tasting.

J. Lohr, one of the new generation of California wineries, planted its first premium vines in 1972, with a limited production of quality wines first offered to the public in 1976. Today, visitors will find many award ribbons proudly displayed on its tasting room walls. Those with limited time in San Jose will find it both convenient and enjoyable to visit J. Lohr, which is less than a mile from the city's center.

Throughout the year, there are a variety of festivals, outdoor concerts and other special events highlighting the San Jose area's wine growing legacy. Tasting are sponsored by local organizations throughout the valley, in addition to those regularly scheduled at the wineries. While many wineries offer free tours of their facilities and sampling at congenial tasting rooms year-round, an advance call is recommended to confirm hours before a visit.

Silicon Valley: Not A Place, But A Phenomenon

 Visitors to California often ask how to find Silicon Valley. Throughout the world, Silicon Valley is as well known as California's Yosemite Valley. Yet, unlike Yosemite, Silicon Valley can not be found on a map. However, a visit to San Jose in Northern California, will put a traveler at the center of the famed valley that is more a phenomenon than a place.

Difficult to capture in words, yet powerful in impact, the drive and creativity spawned by Silicon Valley fuels and challenges high technology industries around the world. The entrepreneurial energy and fierce spirit of innovation and opportunity that are integral characteristics of the Silicon Valley phenomenon promise to thrive as San Jose continues its current redevelopment plans.

The Tech Museum of Innovation opened its ``start-up'' facility in 1990 (then-called the Technology Center of Silicon Valley ``Garage'') and was named one of the top 10 new U.S. attractions by the Weissmann Travel Reports. The permanent site, slated to open in 1997, will enhance the already vibrant image of the Silicon Valley. A dynamic concept in science museums, The Tech will chronicle and demonstrate the rich progression of technology, its applications, and its relentless impact on industry and society.

The high technology boom and silicon chip-based semiconductor industry were born in Santa Clara County around metropolitan San Jose during the 1960s. The industry also gave birth to a new landscape. In a valley spread with orchards thirty years ago, fruit trees made way for high tech industrial parks as the tiny integrated circuit became the County's principal harvest crop.

The actual term ``Silicon Valley'' owes its popularization to the late Don Hoefler, a city of Santa Clara engineer and editor of Microelectronics News. Hoefler used the insiders' phrase in a trade journal article in 1971. The term was a tag describing the region that almost overnight spawned a myriad of companies based on computers, electronics components, software, research, venture capital and all aspects of the technology industry's infrastructure.

Silicon Valley's most dramatic growth occurred during the late 1960s and early 1970s. New electronics firms seemed to spring up overnight bringing dramatic improvements in integrated circuitry, semiconductors, microprocessors, mainframe computers and minicomputers. Santa Clara County's population grew four-fold to keep pace, and low concrete buildings for production and research overran the valley's orchards. Names like Avantek, ASK Computers, Monolithic Memories, Tandem, Amdahl, Four-Phase, Memorex, Rolm, Atari, Information Storage Systems, and a host of others eagerly launched operations during those heady days of the high tech revolution. IBM expanded in south San Jose and Hewlett-Packard occupied numerous facilities in San Jose, Palo Alto, Sunnyvale, and Cupertino.

Like their early compatriots of the technology age, Apple Computer's iconoclastic co-founders, Steve Jobs and Stephen Wozniak, nurtured their first product -- what Jobs and Wozniak called a personal computer -- in a Silicon Valley garage. Ten years later, the personal computer has become a key factor in revolutionizing the way we work and learn. Hard on Apple's heels were IBM, Hewlett-Packard, Commodore, Osborne, Eagle Computers, as well as Sun Microsystems and SGI, and an accompanying pack of venture capitalists and software companies. All were eager to capitalize on the latest trend of entrepreneurial success and promised payoff of fast growth.

Today, with technology ever evolving, Silicon Valley is in the midst of another surge of change. New products are supplanting the old and increased technical capabilities are yielding ever-more sophisticated and intelligent products. The result is an industry modifying and transforming, moving full-speed ahead to meet communication challenges of the 21st century.
