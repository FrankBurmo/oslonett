MOTOWN

THE CITY OF GREAT EXPECTATIONS

The oldest city in the Midwest, Detroit was discovered in 1701 and has since become the center of business and recreation for millions. Encompassing three counties---Wayne, Oakland and Macomb---the Metropolitan Detroit area spans 2,026 square miles and is home to 4.6 million people, with more than one million living within the city.

The downtown area is a true reflection of the diversity between past and present in Detroit. While antique trolley cars circle past the Civic and Renaissance Centers, the Downtown People Mover carries people overhead on a 2.9 mile track around the Central Business District.

Detroit's vibrant 75-acre Civic Center is a riverfront mecca for convention, entertainment, festival and sporting activities. Home to Cobo Conference/Exhibition Center and Arena, Joe Louis Arena, Veteran's Memorial Building, and Philip A. Hart Plaza, the Civic Center is adjacent to some of the area's finest hotels, top-notch restaurants and the Renaissance Center.

In 1989, Cobo Conference/Exhibition Center underwent a $225 million expansion, increasing the facility's exhibit space to 700,000 square feet. The result...one of the nation's largest convention centers.

Renaissance Center is the city's largest office complex with seven gleaming towers of activity located on the Detroit riverfront. The 73-story Westin Hotel is housed within this symbol of downtown development.

Across the street lies the popular riverfront area development, the Robert Millender Center. Opened in September, 1985, the Center features the Omni International Hotel, retail shops and over 300 luxury apartment units.

Long known as the automobile capital of the world, the Detroit area is headquarters to all of the major automobile companies. While much of the area's industry centers around the "horseless carriage," Detroit is more than a smokestack town. Home to many Fortune 1,000 firms, Metropolitan Detroit ranks high among U.S. cities in total spendable income. The Detroit River handles nearly 30 million tons of cargo annually and provides connections to more than 200 overseas ports.

With its access to the Detroit River, the city offers many unique riverfront activities. Just east of the Renaissance Center lies Belle Isle, the 1,000-acre island park jewel. On Belle Isle, visitors can marvel at the nation's oldest fresh water aquarium, visit more than 200 mammals and birds, or simply take a quiet walk along its shore. For nautical lovers, a riverboat cruise is available offering a distinctive look at river's history and breathtaking views of the skyline.

A short walk from the riverfront and the rigors of business is the enjoyment of exciting ethnic nightlife, known to Detroiters as Greektown. A two-block area of authentic Greek food and entertainment, Greektown has become one of the favorite gathering places for Detroiters and visitors alike.

Greektown is also home to The Alley. At one time, The Alley was 18 different free standing warehouses which housed fur tannery operations until the early 1900s. Today, it encompasses five levels of glass, turn-of-the-century pathways and the finest in dining and shopping.

Another center of nightlife in Detroit is the rediscovered and reclaimed Rivertown district, an area dominated by factories, railroad tracks and the bustle of new nightlife found in renovated warehouses and saloons. Whether it's nouvelle American cuisine, good old fashioned cooking or a trendy dance floor, Rivertown offers it all.

Detroit is also a cultural showplace. Five minutes from downtown and adjacent to Wayne State University is the Cultural Center. Consisting of 18 educational institutions including one of the nation's finest art museums--the Detroit Institute of Arts, the Cultural Center allows visitors to walk from building to building all within one area. One can enjoy art and scan the shelves that house over 2.5 million books in the Detroit Public Library; experience Michigan's Underground Railroad movement at the Museum of African American History; be part of oldtime Detroit while strolling the "Streets of Detroit" at the Detroit Historical Museum; be a kid again at the Children's Museum; and marvel at the giant Omnimax theatre at the Detroit Science Center.

Just north of Detroit is another national cultural treasure, the Cranbrook Educational Community. The Institute of Science, Academy of Art Museum, the Cranbrook House & Gardens and Christ Church offer individual tours within the ambience of Cranbrook's picturesque national setting.

Americana at its finest is displayed at Dearborn's Henry Ford Museum and Greenfield Village. While Henry Ford Museum displays the artifacts and implements used in earlier times, Greenfield Village demonstrates how these items were used and how they affected lifestyles. Featured are Thomas Edison's Menlo Park laboratory, Abraham Lincoln's courthouse and the Wright Brothers' workshop.

One of Henry Ford Museum's most popular exhibits, "The Automobile in American Life," opened in November 1987. This permanent exhibition explores the influence the car has had on America through new acquisitions including a 1940's Texaco gas station, a 1960's Holiday Inn motel room, a diner, a drive-in movie theatre and other tidbits of automobile Americana.

The influence the automobile has on the Detroit area goes beyond the area's number one industry. Thousands of visitors each year marvel at the homes of Detroit's greatest auto barons. Meadow Brook Hall, built by Matilda Dodge Wilson, widow of John Dodge, is a 100-room English Tudor home which features among other things, 43 chimneys. The Edsel and Eleanor Ford House was inspired by Cotswold cottage located in Worcestershire, England. Set on 90 acres on Lake St. Clair, the home reflects the Ford's love of art with the original paintings and furnishings intact. In Detroit, Lawrence Fisher's home, now the Bhaktivedanta Cultural Center, features the decor of the Italian Renaissance period and sports one of the area's best vegetarian restaurants, Govinda's. Across town in Dearborn is Edsel's father's house, Henry Ford's Fair Lane Estate. This 56-room Scottish baronial mansion has its own four-story powerhouse and 12-car garage.

Music for every taste abounds in Metropolitan Detroit. The world renowned Detroit Symphony Orchestra plays in historic Orchestra Hall and has a summer festival under the stars at Meadow Brook. Each Labor Day Weekend, jazz greats join local performers for the popular Montreux Detroit Jazz Festival. Music fans are entertained at several area music houses including Music Hall Center and The New Pine Knob.

In Detroit's popular Theater District, the Gem Theatre has people recalling the great times of yesteryear. The intricate filigree ceiling and restored wall panels of red and gold make audiences feel positively regal. The main level features tabletop seating and the balcony level features seats from the Old Ambassador Theatre in St. Louis. While it is the smallest theatre in the downtown district, some say it is the coziest. The theater hosts Broadway revues and plays that are always crowd favorites.

Other renovations to Detroit's diversified entertainment community are the Fox Theatre, a spectacular siamese - byzantine movie palace designed by C. Howard Crane in 1928 that completed an $8 million renovation in 1988, and the Riverfront Music Theatre located in Chene Park on the Detroit River, featuring a 5,000 seat covered amphitheatre.

In 1993, Second City arrived in Detroit. This famous comedy ensemble offers a laugh-filled look at Detroit. Nothing beats the thrill of live theatrical entertainment and Detroit offers a great variety. Broadway productions travel to Detroit year-round and play at the Fisher, Fox, Birmingham and Masonic Theaters. Top-notch productions are presented at the Meadow Brook, Bonstelle and Hilberry theaters. The best of the new and offbeat is performed at the Attic Theater.

Detroit is a great sports town, known for its enthusiastic fans. The Detroit Pistons, who captured back-to-back National Basketball Association Championships in 1989 and 1990, "tip off" at the Palace of Auburn Hills.

The 1984 World Series champions, the Detroit Tigers "hit them out" of historic Tiger Stadium on Trumbull Avenue. At Joe Louis Arena, the Red Wings face hockey opponents from across North America and the Detroit Lions do battle in the Pontiac Silverdome.

The Silverdome became an international sports story when Detroit was selected to host four games of the 1994 World Cup of soccer, marking the first time the World Cup has ever been played indoors.

Lions and tigers of a different kind are found in the Detroit Zoo. The 127-acre park was the first modern zoo in the country to follow a barless exhibit plan throughout the

grounds. Open year-round, the Zoo features a train ride to all corners of the park.

While the attractions of the city are varied and exciting, Detroit is also known for its crowd-gathering special events. Many summer weekends, thousands gather at Hart Plaza for the series of Riverfront Festivals that are the largest and oldest series of festivals in the Midwest. The combination of ethnic food, entertainment and specialty vendors add up to continuous excitement on the riverfront.

Two-thirds of a mile across the Detroit River lies Windsor, Ontario, Canada. To celebrate the friendship and independence of the two nations, the International Freedom Festival is held for two weeks each summer with activities in both countries. The event reaches a peak with the annual fireworks display, the largest in North America.
