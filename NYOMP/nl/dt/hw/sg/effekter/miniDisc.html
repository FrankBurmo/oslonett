Why MiniDisc is Way Cool!

Brief History

During a period of time in 1993, right around the time Sony's brand-new digital medium MD (MiniDisc) made its debut alongside Philips' DCC (Digital Compact Cassette), there was a huge "religious" discussion on rec.audio newsgroup regarding whether one was better than the other, whether they were both destined to doomville, whether history is repeating itself (alluding to cassettes v.s. LP), and whether people should buy them... ad nauseum.

The following is an excerpt of my response to all the unjust MD bashing on the net at the time. Funny thing is to this day, people are still hesitant and holding back on the MiniDisc technology... and for mostly good reasons too may I add. Oh, and what about Philips' DCC? Well, that's been since added to Philips' growing list of failures and flops.

So if you've ever wondered whether MD is for you, just read on. In a nutshell, I'm stoked with my unit and it's been a real joy using it.

Seems like there's a lot of healthy discussion on the topic of MD and
its viability in this age of media bombardment. There's been many
pros and cons discussed and they are all very valid. A few months
ago, before purchasing a Sony MZ-1 MD player, I had many questions
about this mysterious and hyped up digital medium. Is it for real?
Are the articles we read in magazines biased? Now that I'm on the
other side of the fence, having owned one for a few months, I will
attempt to sum up its strengths and weaknesses as objectively as
I can ... in order to educate the curious ones.

First off, the MD technology does indeed use compression ("lossy
algorithm") to fit all that info, up to 74 minutes, onto a surface
area much smaller than its bigger counterpart.  The tradeoff is
degradation in sound quality and subsequent digital to digital
copies will be expected to degrade as well.  However, the noticeability
of this degradation has been the basis for many discussions.  I would
hesitate to say that it sounds, "godawful" as a gentleman previously
has commented.  THis is an unfair statement.  I was able to run some
A/B comparison listening tests to resolve this question for myself.

Several "challenging and familiar" recordings were put to the test.
I specifically chose material that explored the stereo spectrum, 
detailed musical passages w/ subtle percussive instruments at the
periphery, and high quality piano solo music.  I listened carefully for
transients, resolution and overall warmth.   My finding was interesting.

First, my test equipment included Sony MZ-1 MD Player, Sony 1-bit
Discman (high end), and Sony's mid-line ES-series CD player (can't
remember exact model #)  all piped thru the same amplifier and 
judged thru a pair of hi-end headphones.  


RESULTS:

The MD did have a slight bit more noticeable background noise even
when recording direct digital.  It's only noticeable on the quiet dead
space passages of the piano solo pieces .. thru the headphone.  It
did a surprisingly good job w/ the transient passages and the hard
to reproduce acoustic piano sustain and decay.  It was able to
reproduce quite well the periphery percussive instruments but upon
careful listening, did lose a slight bit of "imaging" and depth.  

When compared w/ the 1-bit Sony Discman, this differences became
less noticeable.  The Discman still had a slightly more defined sound...
..but this means, if you're used to a medium to low grade CD player, 
you may find yourself straining to hear the degradation.

In general, the MD did a fine job of reproducing the original source
while maintaining the dynamic range and clarity.  It definitely did
not sound "godawful".   However, the standard caveat is that it is
not fitting for Professional use where lossy is not tolerated.  This
is understandable and expected. 

However, for the general public who is in search of the "next best thing"
in portable digital recording can rest assured that the MD is a
strong alternative.  Now let's spend some time talking about the areas
that MD excels, regardless of how "bells and whistle" these may be.
For every feature that one shoots down as excess, many other can
utilize beneficially.  Let's discuss them and let the judgement fall
upon every individual's unique needs.

The ability to random access can't be overstated for its many 
fringe benefits.  It may sound like marketing hype on paper but
all you have to do is use it a few times, and its convenience should
be clear.  We are all familiar w/ the benefits of Random Access for
playback (CD players) :  instant access, fast preview, shuffle,
fast repeat... and so forth.

However, when random access is considered for record/playback, it's
a much rosier picture.  When it comes time to do "maintenance" on
a CD that has had many disjoint tracks recorded on it, deleting and
reorganizing songs and freeing up space is a cake-walk.  The MD, 
very much like a computer hard disk, will manage where available space
is and "link" all the free spaces into a virtual contiguous memory
for recording.  To the user, it is  transparent.  This means
you can constantly record new selections and delete old unwanted
ones all while not wasting any freed up blank space.  Of course, we
are not talking about some boundary cases (eg: hundreds of little 
musical pieces randomly scattered all over the disc.

The MD hence keeps track of the next available recordable spot on
the disc... you never have to worry about where to start recording
assuming you are not trying to punch in purposely.   The
DAT in this area has an END ID which serves the same purpose...but
once you start deleting tracks in the middle of a tape, maintenance
of free space and continuity can grow uglier.

So let me just conclude by saying that the Pros should definitely 
stick to the old faithful DAT and rightfully so.  However, for the
serious amateur on down, it is unfair to not give MD a try given 
its many advantages.  Words like "godawful" must be used carefully
since it can heavily bias potentially happy owners.

Personally, I consider myself pretty discriminating in my sound
reproduction.  However, I must say that I'm quite happy with the MD
technology because it has enabled me to capture some good live
recordings in a very painless way.   I do use the Random Access
advantage of the MD quite often.  It has made maintenance very
painless.  Of course, this aspect is going to differ from user to
user.   A big plus has been the microphones I've constructed for
my MD player allowing me to record live very well.

Sonically, I will say it is not acceptable for Pro use but very acceptable
for advance amateurs willing to give a little for a lot.  Now whether
it will survive the digital war or not is debatible but I believe it will
prevail over DCC.  Someone said it well on the net... something about
the mass never really too concerned w/ sound quality anyway.  This
I'm afraid I have to agree with.  And those that do appreciate better
sound and live recording won't normally want to mess w/ ACC or
put a dirty tape thru a delicate DCC head.   So the only DCC advantage
of backwards compatibility is a weak one.  On the other hand, the MD's
random access record/playback has no counterpart in the mass market 
today and sonically, it is quite good.  That alone might not cut it 
but it sure has a chance.  

So I'm encouraging netters curious about the MD to give it a test drive.
It is no substitute for DAT but you'll be surprised how good it is.
