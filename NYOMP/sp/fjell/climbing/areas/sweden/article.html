Rock climbing in Sweden.

If you look up Sweden in Walt Unsworth's Encyclopaedia of Mountaineering, you can read the following entry: "Sweden, climbing in. Kebnekaise (2 117 m) in Arctic Sweden is the highest summit, glaciated, but easy to climb and of little interest. In summer, the whole area is heavily infested with midges". It isn't really that bad. True, we don't have as many mountains as our westernly neighbor, Norway, but there are good climbing sites nevertheless. Apart from the mountainous region in the north, there are a number of crags virtually all over the country. Most of these are described in guidebooks published privately or by the local climbing clubs. Some are new, while others are a bit outdated. They are usually available from climbing shops or outdoor equipment shops. Information on newly discovered crags and new route information is published in Bergsport, the official magazine of the Swedish Mountaineering Council, "Svenska Klaetterfoerbundet".
What follows will be a brief overview of the major and/or most classic climbing areas and rocks in Sweden. Most climbing in Sweden takes place on granite or gneiss, which means that polished holds are quite unusual, even in heavily frequented areas. There are only a handful of crags where you can find sedimentary rock, e.g. limestone or quartzite.
Some rocks have certain limitations imposed on them, usually for the protection of nesting birds, but sometimes for other reasons. It us up to you, as a climber, to collect and respect such access limitations!
Southern Sweden
The most classic rock in southern Sweden, Kullaberg, is also the major Danish (sic) crag. It is a peninsula of sorts, extending out into the sea, approximately 30 km north west of the city of Helsingborg. The length of the Kullaberg coastline is almost 10 km, and there are climbable cliffs along a large part of this stretch. There is climbing along both the north and the south coast, mainly within a natural reserve, and the number of routes exceeds 500. Most routes are 15 to 40 meters long. The majority of routes lie in the IV VI range (5.4 5.10), although you will find routes of almost any standard. The climbing is usually well protected, the rock being rich in cracks. The rock is a mixture of amphibolite and diabase, which translates to good friction. The rock on the north coast is subject to heavier frost erosion than the south, which means a greater risk of loose rock.
Even if Kullaberg for a number of years has dominated the southern Swedish climbing scene, new cliffs have been discovered and developed recently; mainly around Haessleholm, Karlskrona and Halmstad.

The West Coast
The crags in Gothenburg lie mainly within the so called Utby-Fjaellbo area, but there are others, e.g. Gardsaas and Lexby, nearby. The cliffs range from 10 to 50 meters and are composed of a rough porphyric granite-gneiss, which really means that the rock is solid, rough and has good friction. The guidebook describes hundreds of routes. Cracks and dihedrals dominate, although a great number of bolted sport climbs have been put up in recent years. The cracks are usually well protected, if you have the strength to place protection. Most routes are quite steep and strenous.
Bohuslaen is a climber's paradise, and the number one area in Sweden. The cliffs lie in a relatively limited area north of Lysekil and Smoegen, west of Munkedal. The climbing started in earnest here in the late 1980's, and although there now are several hundreds of routes here the potential for new routes here, both on established crags and on crags not yet developed or even discovered, is enormous.
The rock here is granite of prime quality. Cracks dominate, although other types of climbing can be found here as well. You will find anything from finger cracks to chimneys, from overhangs to slab climbing. A small number of bolted routes exist, but traditional climbing, i.e. nut protected, dominates. Since most cliffs are rather steep, most routes are accordingly rather difficult. You will have to be able to lead nut protected 5+ (5.8), or preferably higher, in order to find much to do here. The routes range from a few meters up to two or three pitches.
It is difficult to pick just a few crags to mention in particular, but a few worthwhile cliffs are at any rate Hallinden, Vettekullen, Vaelseroed, Brappersberget by Lyse Church, Haeller, Buraasen, Vinternberget, and the more recently discovered Skaelefjaellet and Skyggeberget at Haernaes. Then there's also Moehaettan, famous for it's friction slabs, and the area around Smoegen, where shorter routes and bouldering can be found.

The Eastern part of South Sweden
The island of Gotland is not known for it's climbing, but there are a few possibilities. This is one of the few places in the country where climbing on limestone can be found, though not on the brittle and loose coastal cliffs, but rather on small cliffs in the interior of the island.
In Smaaland there are a number of crags. A few of them have been climbed on since the 1940's, and new ones are discovered every year. The length and character of the routes, as well as the ethics, i.e. how bolts are used, varies from crag to crag. The most developed crags are found around the city of Vaestervik, and up the Ukna Valley towards Aatvidaberg, as well as along the Linkoeping Kisa Hulstfred axis. Climbing can also be found e.g. in the neighborhood of Joenkoeping and Vaernamo.
In the area around Norrkoeping, in the Kolmaarden Forest, a number of cliffs are found. The most well developed ones are the rocks at Lake Aagelsjoen near Aaby. Around a hundred interesting routes of up to 35 meters can be enjoyed here.

Greater Stockholm
The most popular crag south of Stockholm, outside Greater Stockholm, is Simonsberget, also known as Tunaberg, outside Nykoeping. The crag is a few hundred meters in width, and up to 50 meters high. Most routes are not this long, though. Ledges are abundant, and most routes don't have stretches of sustained climbing longer than 10 20 meters. Cracks and dihedrals dominate, but face- and slab climbing is found as well. The routes are mostly well protected with nuts, and on very good granite.
In the area around the capital, Stockholm, there are hundreds of more or less developed crags, and the number of routes probably exceeds 1500. Most crags are found south of the city.
One of the most popular crags is the Vaardberget in Fituna. It has about 50 short routes, and on weekends it is crowded with top rope climbers. For this reason, it's popularity is difficult to understand, especially since it is so close to Groenbrinksberget, one of the best crags in Stockholm. At Groenbrinksberget there are over 30 routes providing climbing at a moderate angle along well protected cracklines. There are also a number of other crags in the neighborhood, neither of which has a large number of routes, but together make this an attractive area. The rocks here are all gneiss.
Outside Huddinge, not far from Vaarby, a number of crags are found. These are quite popular, and understandably so since they are so easy to reach by bus or subway. By far the most popular crag is Haeggsta, where climbers have been active since the 1930's. Over 100 routes of all grades are found here, most of them protected with a mixture of nuts and fixed protection in the relatively compact gneiss. Not far from Haeggsta is Korpberget, a lake-, rather than sea-cliff, which is accessed via rappell. Best avoided in spring because of aggressive nesting sea-gulls. About 5 km south, on the shore of Lake Tullingesjoen, you will find Oernberget, a veritable outdoor gym with it's boltprotected overhanging, or very overhanging, routes.
On the Tyresoe peninsula, approximately 20 km south east of Stockholm, a number of fine crags are located. Most routes here are fairly difficult, but there are a few good routes in the lower grades here, too. Worthwhile crags are: Kloevberget, "Gritstoneberget", Lilla and Stora Traeskbergen, "Doedskalleberget" and the cliffs on Dyviksudde. On a few of the rocks here, as well as on Groenbrinksberget and Oernberget, climbers have been approached even attacked by more or less aggressive neighbors or landowners. Check around in the guidebook, in climbing stores, with the Stockholm Climbing Club, or the neighbors themselves for up-to-date access information. The area around Nacka/Vaermdoe east of Stockholm harbors a number of small, but fine cliffs. One of the larger ones, as well as the main cliff in the area, is Klosterberget in Skevik. You will find some of the finest, steepest, most sustained and difficult routes on this cliff.
North of Stockholm a climber can initially get desperate for lack of cliffs. Needlessly so, for at Runsa, outside Upplands Vaesby, there is a 25 meter high, beautifully situated, crag hosting about 20 routes on good granite. Another beautifully situated crag north of Stockholm is Gaaseborg, nor far from Jakobsberg.

Central Sweden
Not far north of Oerebro the climber will head for Rosendal, where about 100 routes of up to 20 meters in height can be found on rock of good quality (gneiss ?). One of the areas' attractions are the many good boulders there. On the flatlands around Uppsala you have to have very sharp eyes to see any mountains. There are, however, a small number of smaller crags and bouldering areas apart from the urban buildering on the wall of the castle, e.g. Aalandsklipporna, Dalkarlskaerret and Predikstolen. A novel, and interesting alternative for the Uppsala based climber is currently under development. This is an old mining hole i Dannemora, about 40 minutes' drive north-east from Uppsala. Climbing here takes place in a 50 by 150 meter, and 80 meters deep, hole, which is entered by rappell. To climb here, a permit is required, as signs on the fence surrounding the hole informs the visitor.
The most important cliff in Dalarna is doubtlessly Bispbergs klack outside Saeter, about 40 minutes' drive from Falun. Bispberg is unique, at least from the perspective of a central Swedish climber, and few other cliffs in the country are comparable in certain respects. Here you will find a large number of easy and moderate routes, grade 3 6 (5.0 5.10), but about the only thing you won't find are really difficult routes. Bispberg is a 60 80 meter high granite cliff of a couple of hundred meters width. Both face and crack climbing, as well as slab climbing, can be savoured, and most routes are nut protected or protected by a mixture of nuts and fixed pro. It isn't all that steep, about 70 80 degrees, and this is the main reason for the absense of desperates. What makes Bispberg unique is that it is one of the few crags in the country where the average climber can accumulate experience at belay changeovers and other skills required for longer routes in bigger mountains.
Bispberg will be made into a natural reserve, and it is not yet clear whether climbing will be permitted or not in the future.
In western Dalarna, several large cliffs are hidden away. The most well-known one is Hyckjeberget, near Aelvdalen. It is quite big by Swedish standards, about 120 meters high, and several hundred meters wide. The climbing is steep, ranging from 75 degrees to the vertical, and is quite demanding. The routes are sustained and exposed, often with hanging belays from equalized micro-nuts and small wires. Some of the modern routes are equipped with bolts, but most routes require extensive nut-placing skills. A large part of the cliff lies within a natural reserve, where climbing is officially banned, although the few climbers who go there don't seem to take much notice...

North Central Sweden
In the dense forests of Haelsingland, there are a number of crags, although there are bound to be a few hidden gems awaiting discovery. There is, however, really only one crag of more than regional significance, Moerkberget outside Jaervsoe. There one can romp up around 60 different routes, mostly in the 10 30 meter range, and most of them around 5+ to 6 (5.8 to 5.9). On the coast, not far from Sundsvall, lies Brattberget, also known as Nyhamnsberget or Essvik with the Sundsvall climbers. On the 300 meter wide and up to 50 meter high cliff there are a dozen, or so, of quite high quality as well as difficulty. The area around Sundsvall is young from a climbing perspective, but should provide a wealth of climbing possibilities in the future.
Northern Sweden
Jaemtland is probably, along with Bohuslaen, Smaaland and Dalarna, one of the best climbing areas in the country. There are a multitude of cliffs, far more than are actively climbed on. The most important crag is Stuguberget, with about 150 routes of almost any grade you wish. There are several cliffs with a climbable height ranging from 10 to 100 meters. The cliffs are composed of several different kinds of rock, which makes climbing here rather fun and varied. Practially in the city of Oestersund is another crag of importance, Froesoeberget, with 50 or so different routes.
In Jaemtland, you will also find the southernmost mountain climbing in Sweden. In the Sylarna massif, as well as in the Helags and a couple of other places, the routes are of a decidedly alpine character.
Travelling along the coast, north of Sundsvall, the first major crag you run into is Roesaasberget, south-west of Oernskoeldsvik. The cliff is a few hundred meters wide and 100 to 120 meters high, and there are a few dozens of long and varied routes on it. The climber here has to cope with some moss and lichen (very few routes were cleaned by the first ascentionists), as well as enough loose rock to make a helmet a requirement. The odd fixed pin can be encountered, but mostly you place your own protection, both runners and belays. This contributes to the feeling of mountaineering (rather than rock climbing) here.
Blaaberget, 90 km west of Umeaa, has lately become the home crag of the Umeaa climbers, but to describe it as a crag of national importance is not to exaggerate. So far, around 30 different routes of up to 60 meters in length have been put up, but the cliff is 300 meters wide so there's room for more! Further north we run into Doedlarsberget, 80 km from Luleaa. This is also a fairly large crag, with routes of up to two pitches in length.
For someone living in the southern part of the country, the main reason for travelling to the northern part of the country, however, is more likely to be the alpine climbing in the Kebnekaise area than cragging. Despite what Walt Unsworth says about Sweden (climbing in) in The Encyclopaedia of Mountaineering, there is an abundance of alpine routes in the Kebnekaise massif. The rock here is loose, requiring carefulness, but the routes are long enough to require well developed route-finding skills. There are no cable cars there is a 20 km walk just to get to the hut which makes this a rather un-crowded area, a fact, which together with the arctic landscape and midnight sun makes this a special and attractive area in it's own particular way. There are also several interesting winter mountaineering routes here, as well as a number of ice climbs. These are particularly demanding as the days are very, very short in mid winter.

Ice climbing
Ice climbing possibilities vary from year to year, depending on the severity of winter. As a rule of thumb, the further north you go, the more certain the ice buildup.
Some years, there is ice formation on several locations in Smaaland. The area around Lake Aagelsjoen, near Norrkoeping, is the home of a number of good ice climbs, up to grade 6.
Njupeskaer, in the Fulufjaellen mountains in western Dalarna, is the highest waterfall in Sweden, but not the longest ice climb. Climbs here are two to three pitches, from grade 5 and upwards. One or two mixed and thin ice routes have been put up to the left of the main waterfall.
Kaavan, in Funaesdalen, Haerjedalen, sports a long ice climb, usually offering several variations from grade 5 and upwards. The ice is just over 100 meters long, but descent can usually be made in two 50 meter rappells to the right of the ice.
Kloevsjoe, in Jaemtland, offers two large waterfalls, the Fettjeaafallet, which is a proper waterfall in summer, and another to the right of this. Fettjeaafallet is about two full pitches in lenght. The angle is moderate, except for the initial 18 meter vertical pillar. This pillar can be avoided by a deviation on the right, making the route slightly longer than the two pitches. The big icefall on the right, above the deviation mentioned, looks very inviting with it's top 45 by 45 meter vertical wall. This has been the playground for many a climber, and virtually every inch of it has been climbed. To ensure the formation of this ice curtain, local climbers usually redirect the some of the water from the Fettjeaan stream, and unless the redirecting measures are left intact, the ice wall will not be nearly as magnificent as it can be.
