Neighborhoods

San Francisco was thrust from a sheltered Spanish upbringing into the wanton world of the Barbary Coast, which explains how a shy mission belle grew up to be the least conventional of American cities. The only law the Gold Rush rabble recognized was laissez-faire. Under no compulsion to conform to a new culture, San Francisco's immigrants preserved their own -- to the gratification of anyone who has ever prowled the back alleys of Chinatown, basked in the brio of North Beach, caught the spring kimono festival in Japantown or fired a nacho with salsa caliente in the Mission District.

Chinatown

 Benevolent dragons and stone lions adorn the Gateway to Chinatown at Grant and Bush. Erected in 1970, this green and ochre gateway opens to the Chinese capital of the western world. Pacific Bell tops its booths with pagoda roofs. Corn on the cob and lotus roots are bin-to-bin.

Chinatown's 24-block labyrinth of exotic shops, renowned restaurants, food markets, temples and small museums is best explored on foot.

The enormous contributions of the early Chinese to California's mining, fishing and rail industries are profiled at the Chinese Historical Society, 650 Commercial Street. The Chinese Culture Center, 750 Kearny Street offers rotating exhibitions of Chinese arts and crafts. The Pacific Heritage Museum at 608 Commercial Street salutes the contributions of Pacific Basin peoples to the West.

North Beach

 As one observer of the local scene puts it ``Naples is just across the street from Hong Kong.'' Where Grant Avenue crosses Broadway and Columbus, it develops a split personality. The North Beach-Upper Grant Avenue area is an enticing mosaic of cappuccino houses and collectibles stores.

Cradled by Telegraph Hill on the east and Russian Hill on the west, its attractions include cabarets, jazz clubs, galleries, inns, top-notch restaurants and gelato parlors. Washington Square, bordered by the imposing Church of Saints Peter and Paul on Filbert, and by Union, Powell, Stockton and Columbus, is a snug spot for lunching with the local paesani. Nearby delicatessens are brimming with appetizers . . . prosciutto, provolone, mozarella. St. Honore cake and sacripantina are local bakery specialties.

For more information write or telephone: North Beach Chamber of Commerce, 253 Columbus Avenue, San Francisco 94133, 403-0666.

Nob Hill

 Nob Hill's lofty plateau has long been synonymous with the city's elite. The name is derived from ``nabob,'' as in big shot. Wealthy railroad barons and Comstock Lode developers once built lavish mansions there.

Crisscrossed by cable car lines at California and Powell Streets, Nob Hill's cachets include Grace Cathedral, Huntington Park, grand hotels, the exclusive Pacific Union Club and Masonic Temple.

Japantown

 In the ethnic compression chamber that is San Francisco, Japan is only 1.1 miles from downtown. Nihonmachi is well-known to the city's more than 12,000 citizens of Japanese descent. For visitors the focal point is the Japan Center at Post and Buchanan. The five-acre complex of hotels, shops, theaters, sushi bars and restaurants is crowned by a five-tiered pagoda.

Every April the Cherry Blossom Festival offers a double-weekend calendar of events which includes taiko drumming, martial arts, doll-making and calligraphy demonstrations capped by an extraordinary parade of costumed performers.

For more information telephone 922-6776 or send a stamped, self-addressed envelope to Japan Center, 1520 Webster Street, San Francisco 94115.

Fillmore Street

 Near Pacific Heights on Fillmore Street south of Broadway are a number of intimate cafes and restaurants as well as a concentration of upscale clothing, kitchenware and home furnishings stores.

Subtitles are de rigueur at the import film house on Fillmore near Clay and the 2000 to 2200 blocks offer a smattering of shops dealing in vintage clothing, costumes and the ``next-to-new.''

Pacific Heights

 If you live in Pacific Heights, your last name is likely to be Getty. Or your employer is Her Majesty Queen Elizabeth II. Consulates, finishing schools and condominiums share this posh perch with the city's scions.

The house browse is best along the Broadway bluff between Webster and Lyon Streets. Of historical and architectural interest are the Spreckels Mansion, 2080 Washington Street; the Whittier Mansion, 2090 Jackson Street and the Bourn Mansion at 2550 Webster Street.

Where Broderick crosses Broadway, the sidewalk is so steep it's a stairway. Pause on the top step for a view of the Golden Gate Bridge and the hills of Marin beyond.

Clement Street

 In the Richmond District some 3.5 miles from downtown, the Clement Street shopping sector and Geary Boulevard are peppered with the insignia of many cultures. Burger joints and banks co-mingle with local watering holes and small emporiums. Russian bakeries offering piroshki and kulich bread adjoin Greek delis and kosher meat markets. With an estimated 35 percent of the city's Chinese-Americans residing in the area, Chinese food markets, restaurants and dim sum parlors are abundant. Neighborhood landmarks include Holy Virgin (Russian Orthodox) Cathedral at 6222 Geary Boulevard and Temple Emanu-El at Arguello and Lake.

Union Street

 Cow Hollow is old-time vernacular for dairy land west of Van Ness Avenue between Russian Hill and the Presidio. Nowadays the cow's milk in this locality is mainly confined to the caffe latte served by coffeehouses along Union Street's 1500 to 2200 blocks. It's also a flourishing shopping sector with a turn-of-the-century flavor.

Clusters of Victorians have been transformed into shopping compounds. Passages lead between buildings to courtyards bordered by boutiques and galleries, and former stables filled with antiques.

Its mews are crammed with restaurants, pubs and al fresco cafe-delicatessens. A number of popular watering holes make this area a magnet for the singles set; especially the legendary ``Bermuda Triangle,'' at Greenwich and Fillmore.

For more information write or telephone: Union Street Association, Inc., 1686 Union Street, San Francisco 94123, 441-7055.

Mission District

 Mission Street, the district's main paseo, once linked Mission Dolores to the Presidio. Boasting one of the best climates in the city, the Mission District has always been a bustling business and residential community. Today ``Se Habla Espanol'' signs attest to the area's predominantly Hispanic population. The original Levi Strauss factory is still in use at 250 Valencia Street. ``Spontaneous renovation'' has brightened Victorians on streets adjoining Hill and Liberty in the inner Mission. Restaurants, taquerias, Mexican bakeries and specialty shops give 24th Street a particularly simpático air.

For more information write or telephone: Mission Economic Cultural Association, 2899 24th Street, San Francisco 94110, 826-1401

Sacramento Street

 Outer Sacramento Street near Presidio Avenue has blossomed into a shopping sector reminiscent of Union Street. What was formerly a service area for Pacific and Presidio Heights residents is now a browsable, five-block cluster of shops, galleries and boutiques. Interspersed with vintage houses are coffee houses and a theater featuring film imports.

Marina

 Certain people grow up in certain neighborhoods. And stay there. The Marina District is one of those neighborhoods. Developed on the site of the 1915 Panama-Pacific International Exposition, this sunny reserve fronts on Marina Green, where joggers, sunbathers and kite fliers abound. Off Marina Boulevard the streets have a Mediterranean cast to them. Grand stucco houses and flats dominate the landscape. Chestnut Street brims with inviting stores and restaurants.

The Tenderloin

 New Yorkers originally coined the word Tenderloin in the 1800s to describe its teeming wards. In recent years, however, thousands of emigres from Laos, Cambodia and Vietnam have given the 20-square-block district west of Union Square new life. Store façades, restaurant menus and the annual Tet Festival celebrating the Vietnamese New Year bear witness to a burgeoning Southeast Asian population.

Though still very much a ``heads up'' kind of neighborhood -- the Tenderloin has one of the largest concentrations of social service agencies in the state -- the area offers fresh experiences for the palate and food for the soul as well. Residents count as many as 200 restaurants and cafes in the area. Sunday Celebrations at Glide Memorial United Methodist Church, 333 Ellis Street, draw SRO congregations, and a small, but growing, arts community is gaining recognition.

Haight-Ashbury

Along the panhandle of Golden Gate Park, nudged up against Buena Vista Park, lies the former flower power haven, Haight-Ashbury. Twenty-five years after the Summer of Love, the gentrified street scene is apt to be more yuppie with a dash of punk. It's a combination that's produced everything from funky stores filled with kitsch, antiques and secondhand clothing to co-op bakeries and free medical clinics.

Alamo Square

 Shutterbugs have made the view from Alamo Square at Hayes and Steiner Streets classic. Dubbed ``postcard row,'' a prim line of Victorian houses is back-dropped by downtown skyscrapers. One of nine historic districts designated by the Department of City Planning, the area embraces several bed and breakfast inns, a haven for White Russians in the 1930s dubbed ``The Tsar's Embassy'' at 1198 Fulton Street, and a 1904 chateau ``built for the Archbishop himself'' at 1000 Fulton Street.

Van Ness

 The city's widest street, Van Ness Avenue, occupies a niche in local lore as ``the street that saved the city.'' It is here that firefighters checked the spread of the fire ignited by the 1906 quake. Formerly ``Auto Row,'' the street now offers trendy restaurants, retail stores, movie houses and condos. Polk Street, Van Ness' campier eastern neighbor combines the silly with the serviceable. Fanning out from the Polk-California intersection are top drawer restaurants, antique and gift stores, movie houses and boutiques where you can be outfitted for a safari or select an antique kimono.

Western Addition

As the city expanded westward past Van Ness Avenue, the Western Addition came to define an area of new growth. Today it embraces three historic districts: Webster Street, Bush Street-Cottage Row and Alamo Square, all renowned for their Victorian architecture. A thriving Japanese-American community and the Center for African and African American Art and Culture speak to a heritage that is rich and varied. Five other resident organizations that are symbols of the historical black presence in San Francisco are housed in the center as well.

Noe Valley

 Flanked by stately palms marching majestically over the ribboning hills of Dolores Street and nestled beneath Twin Peaks and Diamond Heights, Noe Valley is a delightful discovery. Once an oasis of farms and streams, Noe Valley today is a sunny rainbow quilt of vibrantly-colored Victorian homes, gardens and shops both quaint and cutting edge.

Virtually alone among the City's neighborhoods, Noe Valley was spared the destruction of the 1906 earthquake. The beauty of old San Francisco still reveals itself in wisteria-trellised walkways and Continental cafes. The heart of this remarkable ``Little Village,'' 24th Street boasts a kaleidoscopic array of shops, public houses, outdoor cafes and restaurants.

The Castro

Upper Market near Castro, San Francisco's ``Gay Mecca,'' was brightened in the mid-80s with the ``Path of Gold'' lamp posts that illuminate lower Market. Gay entrepreneurs have transformed some of the 1880s Victorians into imaginative boutiques, gay bookstores and bars. Just one block from the Muni Metro's Castro Street station, the Names Project at 2362 Market Street is the home of the AIDS memorial quilt. Built in 1922 the Castro Theater, 429 Castro Street, survives as one of the last grand movie palaces, featuring revivals and pre-film concerts on the mighty Wurlitzer.

South of Market

 Known as ``south of the slot'' to old-timers and dubbed SoMa by those who see a similarity to New York City's trendy SoHo, South of Market is a two-square-mile grid of broad one-way streets and narrow alleys. Roughly bounded by Market Street, The Embarcadero, China Basin and Division Street, SoMa is more like an attitude. Lower rents have attracted speculators, immigrants and the avant-garde. Dozens of restaurants, nightclubs, galleries and experimental theatres flourish here. Bargain hunters discover factory outlets and a wholesale flower mart. Interior designers frequent the showrooms of Showplace Square while the Concourse Exhibition Center hosts a multitude of special events. Located at Third and Howard Streets, Moscone Center, the city's major meeting facility, is crowned by the Center for the Arts.
