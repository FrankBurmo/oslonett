Rock Climbing Areas In Greece

Meteora

This is a must stop for any climber who happens to be in Greece. The setting is great---rural Greece, ancient monasteries atop large pinnacles, high (snowy) mountains nearby, etc. This was the setting for an old (Sean Connery) James Bond movie---the one in which the bad guy hammers (with the butt end of his pistol) out a piton attached to a rope supporting our hero...

The rock is a conglomerate, reminiscent of Pinnacles in California. The aggregate consists of pebble size stones to 500 kg boulders. Usually, these are smooth and polished. Mostly, you climb these things or the holes left when they popped out of their placements. Some of the routes look like dry river beds that have rocks that have been glued in place, and were then tilted up; sometimes you do long runouts on melon sized rocks stuck to a nearly vertical wall.

Many early climbs were established by East Germans who were attracted there because pictures of the area look like their own Elbesandstein. Most routes are bolted face climbs ranging from low angle slabs to vertical or slightly overhanging faces, water grooves, and corners. The style of many of the older routes is distinctly East German---large ring bolts at the belays, long (and often unnecessary) runouts, sometimes followed by (or following) bolt ladders on sections that are only a little harder. Many of the classic routes are very easy (at 5.8 and under), but the first visit will still be a thrill, since a newby won't have a clue as to which pebbles will pop. (The trade routes tend to be more stable, since many of the loose pebbles have already been removed by earlier ascents.)

There is a guide (Hasse and Stutte, I think, or maybe Stutte and Hasse), but it is out of date for newer climbs. Also, it only lists the first ascent, so you won't know if the 5.9 A0 climb it lists has since gone free at 5.10a, 5.12a, or whether it has gone free at all. The guide touts a chalkless ethic for the area and claims that the Greeks are unalterably opposed to its use, but the best Greek climbers use chalk--at least on harder routes--so don't take the guide too seriously. On the plus side, the guide has a star rating system for route quality, has detailed maps of the area, is attractively illustrated, and is worth the price just as an adornment to your coffee table.

There are campgrounds and rooms in Kastraki---less than a mile from the trains and buses in Kalambaka. The approaches by foot are as little as 2 minutes from the north end of town, i.e. you don't need a car. Camp fees are high, but the facilities are excellent. We stayed at Vrachos Campground; the bathhouse has marble steps, tile floors and walls, very hot water, and is cleaner than the toilets in most hotels I've seen.

Both Kastraki and Kalambaka have restaurants, bakeries, stores, and a well developed tourist industry. Kastraki is charming, Kalambaka is more commercial. In July-August, both towns are packed with tourists visiting the monasteries.

On the map, look for Kalambaka or Meteora. About half way between Volos (Aegean port) and Igoumenitsa (Adratic port). I think there is an airport in Larissa. Rail and bus connections to Athens and other points to the south.

The summer temperatures are too hot for much climbing, but late September is supposed to be great (no tourists, nice weather). Easter week is the most crowded time for climbing---queues on lots of the better routes and occasional disappearances of packs and gear from the bases of routes are symptomatic (thefts appear to be due to foreign visitors, NOT Greek locals).

All in all, Meteora is a nice climbing experience.
